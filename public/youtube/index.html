The short version

A proof is not a story. A proof says: here is the thing, here is what it does, look. A story says: here is what I believed, here is what went wrong, here is what it cost me to find out, and here is what I believe now.

The hero’s journey was shrunk to YouTube length before YouTube existed. Dan Harmon drew his eight-beat circle for five-minute Channel 101 shows, years before Community. Kenn Adams boiled the same shape into seven sentences for improv, the ones Pixar later made famous. Either version fits a six-minute video with a beat every forty-five seconds, or a fifteen-second short with one beat and nothing else.

Your own channel has already run the experiment. The hidden-agenda video (July 2026) was written as beats before you recorded it, and it is the only cut to pass quality control with zero failures and zero warnings. The two videos from 5 August were recorded without a treatment, and both were parked. Same presenter, same editor, same tools. The difference was whether the story existed before the camera rolled.

What a proof video is missing is always one of three beats.

  1. The call. What went wrong, and who or what did it.
  2. The cost. What it took, what it broke, what else it caught.
  3. The change. What you believe now that you did not at the start.

The test is simple. Can you write the video as seven sentences? If the sentence after “But one day” is empty, you have a demo. Park it, or find the story it came from.

The spine

Seven sentences

Kenn Adams’s story spine. Fill it in for any video, yours or anyone’s. The leads are fixed; you write the rest. Three worked examples are loaded below to start from. It saves in your browser as you type.

Read back

Notice what the spine refuses to let you skip. “But one day” is the call: if nothing happened, there is no sentence. “Until finally” forces a result and a price in the same breath. “Ever since then” makes you say what changed. A tool demo written as a spine reads: once upon a time there was a tool, every day it did things, but one day, nothing. That is the diagnosis.

Two ways

The same video, told as a proof and as a story

Identical material: a linter you built, four rules, a CI job. The left column is the video you would have made a year ago. The right column is the one you made. Read the timestamps. The story version is longer and people stay for it, because every minute answers a question the previous minute asked.

The proof version is not wrong. It is a reference: somebody who already wants the tool can watch it and install it. But nobody who does not already want the tool will get past the first line, because the first line is “I want to show you a thing.” The story version opens with a crime, and you stay to find out whose.

The beats

Eight beats on a clock

Harmon’s eight, mapped to the kind of video you make: a thing built, a tool fought, a lesson learned. Each beat has the question it answers, what it looks like in a proof video, the trap, the camera, and the hidden-agenda example. Set the length and the clock tells you where each beat lands.

    The rules

    Six rules for a channel that films AI

    These fall out of the beats and out of your own numbers. None of them are taste. Each one has a video behind it that worked or did not.

    The AI is never the mentor

    It is the shapeshifter. Villain when it invents a rule, ally when it ships, and you do not know which until the beat lands. Your top shorts are all Stu versus the tool, with receipts. Frame every proof that way and the grievance does the hooking for you.

    Want versus need

    The want is “make the thing work.” The need is the lesson. Write the need as one sentence before you record, the way you wrote “agents can’t read intent, only instructions,” and put it at beat 8. If you cannot write the sentence, you have a demo.

    Beat six is the one you skip

    What did it cost? The prod incident. The five out of five. The host that ran out of memory. Cost is what makes the lock matter and what makes viewers take a side in the comments. A video with no cost reads as an advert.

    Open on two, four or five. Never one.

    Cold open on the grievance, the fight, or the result with a rewind. Never on the ordinary world, and never on a screen of code. Face, or a striking picture. The belief at beat 1 gets said afterwards, in one line, once they already care.

    The lock happens on camera

    Merge the PR live. Rotate the secret and watch the old one die. Run it on the real estate. Something irreversible while the camera rolls is the difference between a claim and a scene. “I merged it earlier” is a summary.

    Chaos about the process, never the craft

    The swearing, the wrong turn, the rant at the model: keep all of it, it is the retention. The build itself never looks incompetent. Matty Matheson burns the pan and still cooks better than you. That split is the whole channel.

    Shorts

    A short is one beat

    Do not compress a journey into sixty seconds. Take one beat out of it and let it stand alone. Your own numbers say which beats work as shorts and which do not, and the pattern holds across months: moments win, explanations lose.

    Titles are beat two

    A title that describes the video is a label. A title that states the grievance is a side, and people click to take one. Five rewrites, using your own videos and the beats you have banked.

    The sheet

    No treatment, no record

    This is the page you fill in before a shoot. It is the eight beats plus the four things the beats do not cover: the need sentence, the cold open, the lock, and the forward hook. If beats two, six or eight are empty it tells you so, because that is the demo test and it is cheaper to fail it here than in the edit. It saves in your browser. Copy it out as text and paste it wherever the treatment lives.

    Exercises

    Turn these proofs into stories

    Four beats you have already banked, written the way you would have filmed them a year ago. Write the spine for each. When you are done, open the model answer. It is one way it could go, not the way; if yours has a better villain or a worse cost, yours wins. The fifth exercise is whatever you built last.
